This myth, told most fully in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, explains the turning of the seasons through the story of Persephone's abduction. While Persephone, daughter of Demeter, was gathering flowers in a meadow, the earth opened and Hades, god of the underworld, rose in his chariot and carried her down to be his queen — an act done with the consent of her father Zeus but without her mother's knowledge.

Demeter, goddess of grain and harvest, wandered the earth in grief searching for her daughter, and in her sorrow she withheld her gifts: the fields grew barren, nothing sprouted, and humankind faced starvation. To end the famine Zeus commanded Hades to release Persephone, but Hades first induced her to eat a few seeds of a pomegranate — the food of the dead — which bound her to the underworld. As a result Persephone could not remain wholly above: it was decreed that she spend a portion of each year with Hades below and the rest with her mother.

When Persephone is in the underworld, Demeter grieves and the earth grows cold and lifeless — the barren months of autumn and winter — and when her daughter returns, Demeter rejoices and the world bursts again into growth — the fertile spring and summer. The myth was also central to the Eleusinian Mysteries, which drew from Persephone's descent and return a promise of hope for initiates beyond death.
