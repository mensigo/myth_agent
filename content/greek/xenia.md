Xenia is the ancient Greek concept of guest-friendship: the sacred code of hospitality that governed the relationship between hosts and guests, and one of the most important social and religious values in Greek myth and life. Under xenia a host was obliged to welcome a stranger, offer food, shelter, and protection, and refrain from prying into the guest's identity or business until they had been fed; the guest, in turn, was expected to be courteous, not to overstay or abuse the welcome, and often to exchange gifts. Bonds of xenia could pass down through generations and even prevent former guests from fighting one another in war.

Xenia was held to be under the direct protection of Zeus, worshipped in this role as Zeus Xenios, guardian of guests and strangers — so that to violate hospitality was not merely rude but an offense against the gods. Many myths turn on this principle: the Trojan War itself is set off by Paris, a guest of Menelaus, who repays his host's hospitality by carrying off Helen, a grave breach of xenia that the gods punish. In the Odyssey, good hosts like the Phaeacians and King Alcinous are contrasted with violators such as the Cyclops Polyphemus, who eats his guests, and the suitors who abuse Odysseus's household — all of whom meet punishment. Xenia thus works in Greek myth as a moral test that distinguishes the civilized from the savage.
